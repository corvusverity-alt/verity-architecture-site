Compounding

Compounding: The Quiet Engine That Changes Everything

•Overview

Complete this worksheet before moving to Chapter 4. Doing the math by hand once is worth more than reading a table ten times.

1STEP 1

Run the formula for yourself Choose a starting amount — $1,000, $5,000, or whatever is meaningful to you. Calculate its value over 10, 20, and 30 years at 5%, 8%, and 10% using the formula: Future Value = Principal × (1 + Rate)^Time. Use your phone calculator. Write the numbers down. Notice when the curve begins to accelerate.

Your notes & entries
2STEP 2

Apply the Rule of 72 to your current returns What is the approximate annual return you are currently earning across your portfolio — or what return do you expect from the system you are building? Divide 72 by that number. How many years to double? Is that acceptable given your time horizon?

Your notes & entries
3STEP 3

Calculate the cost of one interruption Identify one amount you have removed from a compounding portfolio in the past, or one amount you are considering removing. Apply the formula: what would that amount be worth in 20 years at 8%? That number is the real cost of the interruption, not the face value of the withdrawal.

Your notes & entries
4STEP 4

Identify your compounding timeline How many years do you have before you need to draw on this capital? Write this number down. Now look at the compounding tables in this chapter and find what happens to your starting amount over that exact time period at your expected rate. This is your most realistic financial target.

Your notes & entries
5STEP 5

List your compounding interruptors Write down every behaviour or structure in your financial life that is currently interrupting compounding. Common answers: paying high fees, holding tax-exposed investments in the wrong account, making emotional trades during corrections, maintaining insufficient protection capital (which forces selling growth assets in emergencies). Each item on this list is an area this book will address.

Your notes & entries
◆Reflection Prompts

Write your answers. These are not rhetorical.

◆ If compounding is the most powerful force in personal finance, what specific thing in my current financial structure is most reducing its power?
◆ Using the Early Emma example: have I started early enough? If not, what is the real numerical cost of my delay, and what is the most efficient way to close the gap?
◆ What was the last amount I withdrew from a long-term compounding account for a non-essential reason? What is that amount worth in 25 years at 8%?
◆ At my current savings rate and expected return, when does the compounding line start to bend for me? Have I calculated this?
◆ What is one financial habit I can automate this month that directly feeds compounding — a regular contribution, a fee reduction, a structural improvement — without requiring willpower every time?
◆ If someone asked me to explain compounding to a 16-year-old in two minutes, could I do it clearly and compellingly enough to change how they think about money?

The Hidden Costs

The Hidden Costs: MERs, Expenses, and the Silent Leak

•Overview

The fee audit is one of the most consistently impactful exercises any investor can complete. Most people who do it make at least one significant change. Do it once thoroughly and then schedule it annually.

1STEP 1

Build Your Fee Inventory List every fund, ETF, mutual fund, or managed product you currently own. For each one, find and write down the MER or expense ratio or OCF. Use the fund's own website, the Key Investor Information Document, or your brokerage's fund details page. If you cannot find it in five minutes, call the fund provider and ask.

Your notes & entries
2STEP 2

Calculate the Annual Dollar Cost For each holding, multiply your current balance by the MER. This is the annual fee in dollars. Sum the total. Most investors are surprised by this number. Write it down without softening it.

Your notes & entries
3STEP 3

Find the Lower-Cost Equivalent For each fund, spend ten minutes searching whether a lower-cost fund provides substantially similar exposure. In most cases, an equivalent index ETF exists at a fraction of the cost. List the alternative and the potential annual savings.

Your notes & entries
4STEP 4

Calculate the All-In Cost Add your platform fee, any advisor fee, and estimated trading costs to your MER total. This is your true annual cost of investing. Apply it to your total portfolio. Is this number acceptable given what you are receiving in return?

Your notes & entries
5STEP 5

Make a Decision on Every High-Cost Holding For each holding with an MER above 0.75%, write a sentence: either justify why it earns its premium, or write down when and how you will replace it with the lower-cost alternative. Inertia is not a justification. If it stays, it stays for a written reason.

Your notes & entries
◆Reflection Prompts

Write your answers down.

◆ What is the MER of the largest fund I currently own? When did I last check it?
◆ If I calculate my total annual fee cost in dollars — across all holdings and all platforms — is that number something I have previously known? Should I have known it?
◆ For the most expensive fund I own, can I articulate specifically what I am paying for that a lower-cost alternative does not provide?
◆ Am I holding any legacy products I know I should replace but have not because switching feels uncomfortable? What is that discomfort costing me per year?
◆ If my portfolio doubles in the next ten years and I still own the same high-cost products, what will my annual fee cost be in dollar terms?

The 3:5:7 Rule

The 3:5:7 Rule: A Framework for Every Trade

•Overview

This worksheet builds the discipline infrastructure for the 3:5:7 Rule before you need it under pressure.

1STEP 1

Define Your Portfolio's 3% Threshold Write down your current total portfolio value. Calculate 3% of that value. This is your maximum capital at risk on any single trade. Write the number somewhere visible near your trading workspace. Recalculate every quarter as the portfolio grows.

Your notes & entries
2STEP 2

Write a Pre-Trade Brief Template Create a simple template — digital or paper — with four fields: Thesis, Thesis Break Point, Position Size Calculation, and Target with R:R Ratio. Commit to completing this template before entering any active or tactical position. No exceptions.

Your notes & entries
3STEP 3

Review Your Current Active Positions For every active trade or tactical position you currently hold, attempt to write the pre-trade brief as if you were entering today. Can you still articulate the thesis? Is the R:R still above 5:1? Is the thesis break point still valid? If any position fails this exercise, it deserves an honest review.

Your notes & entries
4STEP 4

Set Your 7-Week Review Calendar For every active position, mark a date seven weeks from today in your calendar as a 'thesis review.' At that date, pull out the pre-trade brief and compare it to current reality. Be honest. The review is the most important discipline habit in active investing.

Your notes & entries
5STEP 5

Identify Which Portfolios the Rule Governs Using the table in this chapter, write down which of your current or planned portfolio sleeves will be governed by the full 3:5:7 standard. For the remaining sleeves, note which review framework applies instead. The rule needs a scope — everything within scope follows it strictly.

Your notes & entries
◆Reflection Prompts

Write your answers down.

◆ Think of the worst trade you have ever made. Would it have passed the pre-trade brief test? Which criterion would it have failed?
◆ What is the largest single position I currently hold as a percentage of my total portfolio? Does the position size reflect a 3% maximum risk calculation, or did I size it based on conviction?
◆ For each active position I hold right now, can I write one sentence stating the original thesis? Can I identify the point at which that thesis is wrong?
◆ How many of my current positions have a documented exit plan? How many were entered with only an entry plan?
◆ When I have sold positions in the past, what typically triggered the sell decision — a pre-planned rule, an emotional reaction, or something else?

Risk vs. Reward

Risk vs. Reward: The Math Nobody Teaches You

•Overview

These exercises are designed to make the chapter's mathematics personally relevant.

1STEP 1

Calculate EV on Your Last Three Trades For the last three positions you opened or are considering, write down your honest probability of winning and losing, the expected gain, and the expected loss. Apply the EV formula. Were these positive or negative expected value decisions? What does that tell you?

Your notes & entries
2STEP 2

Build Your Personal Recovery Table Using the recovery formula, calculate the gain required to recover from losses of 10%, 20%, 30%, 40%, and 50% on your current total portfolio value. Write the dollar amounts — not percentages. The dollar amounts make the stakes concrete in a way percentages do not.

Your notes & entries
3STEP 3

Define Your Maximum Drawdown Tolerance Write down the maximum percentage decline in your total portfolio that you could experience and still hold — not sell, not panic, not change strategy — for a full 12 months. Be honest. Now calculate what asset allocation (equities/bonds/cash) historically produces a maximum drawdown near that number. Does your current allocation match your honest tolerance?

Your notes & entries
4STEP 4

Run a Correlation Audit List your five largest holdings. For each pair, estimate whether their correlation is high (they tend to move together), low (they are largely independent), or negative (they tend to move oppositely). If most pairs are high correlation, your portfolio is less diversified than it appears. Identify one genuinely low-correlation asset you could add.

Your notes & entries
5STEP 5

Calculate Your Sharpe Ratio Using your portfolio's actual returns over the past 1 to 3 years, the standard deviation of those returns (many brokerages provide this), and the current risk-free rate, calculate your Sharpe Ratio. Compare it to 0.5 (acceptable), 1.0 (good), and 2.0 (excellent). If it is below 0.5, investigate whether the portfolio is taking more risk than the return justifies.

Your notes & entries
◆Reflection Prompts

Write your answers down.

◆ For my three largest positions, have I ever explicitly calculated the expected value at the time of entry? If not, do I have any rigorous basis for saying those were good decisions versus lucky ones?
◆ What is the largest drawdown I have personally experienced in a portfolio? What did I actually do — hold, sell, add? Was that response consistent with what I would have predicted about myself before it happened?
◆ If I learned tomorrow that my portfolio had an 80% correlation between its top five holdings, what would I change? Have I ever checked?
◆ Do I think about risk as 'what might go wrong' or as a number I can calculate before it happens? What is the practical difference between those two approaches?
◆ What is the one position in my current portfolio that, if it moved against me severely, would be difficult to recover from? Is that position sized for its actual risk or for its perceived promise?

Fortress 200

Fortress 200: The Tax-Efficient Core

1STEP 1

Find Your Current Marginal Tax Rate Use the table in this chapter to identify your approximate combined federal + provincial marginal rate. If you are near a bracket boundary, identify both the current rate and the next bracket. This rate is the refund percentage on every RRSP contribution. If you are unsure, log into your CRA My Account — your most recent Notice of Assessment shows your available RRSP room and your prior year income.

Your notes & entries
2STEP 2

Decide: RRSP First or TFSA First? Apply the rule: if your current marginal rate is likely higher than your retirement rate, RRSP first. If you are at low income and expect significant income growth, TFSA first — save RRSP room for higher-income years. Exception: if you hold US dividend-paying investments, those belong in the RRSP regardless of income level because the withholding gap applies at every bracket.

Your notes & entries
3STEP 3

Set Your Contribution Amount Alex's template: Start with $50–$100/month. Increase by $25/month each year as income grows. Jordan's template: Maximise the RRSP contribution immediately. Set up automatic monthly contributions so the full $18,000 is deposited by year-end. In both cases: treat the contribution as non-negotiable — the same way a utility payment is non-negotiable.

Your notes & entries
4STEP 4

Schedule Your Refund Reinvestment The tax refund arrives after filing — typically April to June. Set a calendar reminder for the day the refund arrives: immediately transfer it to the RRSP as the following year's contribution. This one action, repeated annually, adds 15–43% more capital to the Fortress depending on income level. It is the highest-return financial action available in that calendar month.

Your notes & entries
5STEP 5

Select the Fortress Positions in Priority Order Priority 1: US dividend ETF (VFV, XUS, or ZSP — treaty rate protection applies at all income levels). Priority 2: Bond ETF (ZAG or VAB — interest sheltered from marginal rates). Priority 3: REIT ETF (VRE or ZRE — distribution income sheltered). Priority 4: Preferred share ETF (CPD or HPR). Priority 5: As the account grows — individual US dividend equities, international income positions, completing the 200-position structure.

Your notes & entries
6STEP 6

Set Your Annual Review Date Once per year, same date: review Fortress 200 positions, rebalance toward target asset class weights, confirm all positions continue to qualify under the income-generation criteria, and add the annual contribution. The review takes a few hours. It is the only active management the Fortress requires.

Your notes & entries
◆Reflection Prompts

Write your answers down.

◆ What is your current marginal tax rate? Have you looked this up, or are you estimating? How does knowing the exact rate change the value you assign to each RRSP contribution?
◆ If you are at a lower income level today, do you expect your income to grow significantly in the next five to ten years? Does that expectation change whether TFSA-first or RRSP-first is the correct strategy right now?
◆ How much unused RRSP contribution room have you accumulated? What would the tax refund on that unused room be if you contributed to it in a high-income year? What is the cost of leaving it unused each year?
